        <sec>
          <title>Background</title>
          <p>Glucosinolate breakdown products are volatile, therefore good candidates for insect fumigants. However, although they are insecticidal, the mode of action of such natural products is not clear. We studied the insecticidal effect of these compounds as fumigants, and monitored the production of carbon dioxide by the insects as a probe to the understanding of their mode of action.</p>
        </sec>
        <sec>
          <title>Results</title>
          <p>The fumigation 24-h LC<sub>50</sub> against the house fly (<italic>Musca domestica</italic> L.) of allyl thiocyanate, allyl isothiocyanate, allyl cyanide, and l-cyano-2-hydroxy-3-butene was 0.1, 0.13, 3.66, and 6.2 Î¼g cm<sup>-3</sup>, respectively; they were 0.55, 1.57, 2.8, and &gt; 19.60 Î¼g cm<sup>-3</sup>, respectively, against the lesser grain borer (<italic>Rhyzopertha dominica</italic> Fabricius). The fumigation toxicity of some of the glucosinolate products was very close to or better than that of the commercial insect fumigants such as chloropicrin (LC<sub>50</sub>: 0.08 and 1.3 Î¼g cm<sup>-3</sup> against <italic>M. domestica</italic> and <italic>R. dominica</italic>, respectively) and dichlorovos (LC<sub>50</sub>: &lt; 0.02 and 0.29 Î¼g cm<sup>-3</sup> against <italic>M. domestica</italic> and <italic>R. dominica</italic>, respectively) in our laboratory tests. Significantly increased CO<sub>2</sub> expiration was found in insects exposed to the vapor of allyl isothiocyanate, allyl thiocyanate and allyl isocyanate. Allyl isothiocyanate was also found to increase the CO<sub>2</sub> expiration of the American cockroach (<italic>Periplaneta americana</italic> L.).</p>
        </sec>
        <sec>
          <title>Conclusions</title>
          <p>Glucosinolate breakdown products have potential as biodegradable and safe insect fumigants. They may act on the insect respiratory system in their mode of action.</p>
        </sec>

      <title>Background</title>
      <p>Plants in the family Cruciferae, such as cabbage (<italic>Brassica olerecea</italic>), mustard (<italic>Sinapis alba</italic>) and crambe (<italic>Crambe abyssinica</italic>) produce glucosinolates which are secondary metabolites that can help plants defend themselves against phytophagous insects, fungi and other pests. Glucosinolates are hydrolyzed by thioglucosidase (myrosinase) enzymes to yield an aglycone which undergoes non-enzymatic rearrangements to produce organic isothiocyanates, thiocyanates, nitriles and other products [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B1">1</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B2">2</xref>], although non-enzymatic thermal degradation of glucosinolates may produce chemical species identical to products of the enzymatic hydrolysis [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B3">3</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B4">4</xref>]. The chemical structures of the hydrolysis products (breakdown products) of glucosinolates vary depending on the precursors in different plant species, and different endogenous or exogenous factors such as pH and ferrous ions [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B2">2</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B5">5</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B6">6</xref>]. Glucosinolates and their breakdown products have been the focus of many studies because of the possibility of using them as natural pesticides [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B7">7</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B8">8</xref>]. However, it is considered that glucosinolates themselves possess limited biological activity until they are hydrolyzed [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B9">9</xref>]. The breakdown products are generally relatively small molecules which make many of them volatile. Many of these volatiles have been shown to act as attractants for certain insects seeking food or egg-laying sites rather than direct insecticidal activity [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B10">10</xref>-<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B14">14</xref>]. Allyl glucosinolate (sinigrin) breaks down in soils to allyl isothiocyanate and allyl cyanide, and these breakdown products and glucosinolate-containing plant tissue have been suggested for use in controlling soil-borne plant pests [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B9">9</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B15">15</xref>-<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B19">19</xref>]. Despite the many efforts that have been devoted to exploring glucosinolates and their breakdown products as naturally occurring pesticides, few products have been marketed commercially. 2-(2-Butoxyethoxy)ethyl thiocyanate (Lethane 384<sup>Â®</sup>) and isobornyl thiocyanoacetate (Thanite<sup>Â®</sup>) were marketed as insecticides, and methylisothiocyanate (Vorlex<sup>Â®</sup>) as a fumigant, although their discovery may or may not have been directly related to glucosinolates. The thiocyanates (Lethane 384 and Thanite) were discontinued by their manufacturers in 1984 [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B20">20</xref>] and methylisothiocyanate, the closest relative of the glucosinolate breakdown products, was also banned in 1991 [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B21">21</xref>].</p>
      <p>Harvested crops including seeds, grains and cereals suffer a loss of at least 10% from insect pests during storage. Losses of 30% are common throughout large areas of the world [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B22">22</xref>]. Many chemical fumigants such as ethylene dibromide and methyl bromide that have been used to control these stored grain insects are being banned or facing phase-out. There are clearly needs for alternative fumigants.</p>
      <p>We have reported the insecticidal activity of the glucosinolates in crambe in our previous study, and found that allyl, 3-butenyl, 4-pentenyl, 4-hydroxybenzyl and 2-hydroxy-3-butenyl glucosinolate were the major glucosinolate components in crambe seed meal [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B8">8</xref>]. We also found that 2-hydroxy-3-butenyl glucosinolate was the predominant glucosinolate among the crambe glucosinolates, and its major breakdown product (&gt;95% of total glucosinolate) was 1-cyano-2-hydroxy-butene (CHB) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B8">8</xref>]. CHB and other crambe glucosinolate breakdown products were found to have fumigation toxicity against insects in our early study [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B8">8</xref>].</p>
      <p>Different fumigants have different insecticidal mode of actions, and some fumigants are known to affect the respiratory function of insects [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B20">20</xref>]. Although it has been reported that the CO<sub>2</sub> expiration of <italic>Tenebrio molitor</italic> was reduced by feeding glucosinolate-containing rapeseed meal [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B24">24</xref>], there are no previous studies on the effect of the glucosinolate breakdown products on insect respiration.</p>
      <p>In this paper we report the insecticidal activity of crambe-related glucosinolate breakdown products as fumigants and the effect of these chemicals on the respiratory function of insects. The latter is achieved by measuring the CO<sub>2</sub> emission of the insects that are exposed to the chemicals. The fumigation effects of the breakdown products were compared and discussed together with the known fumigants dichlorovos and chloropicrin. The effect of the glucosinolate breakdown products on CO<sub>2</sub> emission was also compared with that of hydrogen cyanide and rotenone.</p>
    </sec>

      <title>Results and Discussion</title>
      <p>The house fly (<italic>Musca domestica</italic>) and the lesser grain borer (<italic>Rhyzopertha dominica</italic>) were chosen in the laboratory toxicity test, based on the availability and their significance as pests. In fact, glucosinolate-containing extracts from crambe were found to be toxic to the larvae of the house fly [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B8">8</xref>]. In the present study, we have found that some of the individual glucosinolate breakdown products from crambe were insecticidal toward <italic>M. domestica</italic> as potent fumigants. The respective LC<sub>50</sub> of allyl thiocyanate (ATC), allyl isothiocyanate (AITC) and allyl isocyanate(AIC) were 0.1, 0.13 and 0.63 Î¼g cm<sup>-3</sup> (Table <xref ref-type="table" rid="T1">1</xref>). This is in the same potential range of the toxicity of chloropicrin (0.08 Î¼g cm<sup>-3</sup>), a commercial fumigant. CHB, the major breakdown product of crambe glucosinolates was not as potent a fumigant as others. Its LC<sub>50</sub> was 6.2 Î¼g cm<sup>-3</sup> (Table <xref ref-type="table" rid="T1">1</xref>).</p>

      <p>The high fumigant toxicity of the glucosinolate breakdown products against the lesser grain borer may have important economic impact on stored grain insects management. These naturally occurring chemicals were also insecticidal as fumigant on other stored grain insects such as the sawtoothed grain beetle (<italic>Oryzeaphilus surinamensis</italic> L.) and the red flour beetle (<italic>Tribolium castaneum</italic> Herbst) (Tsao, et al., unpublished data, 1997).</p>
      <p>Long-term fumigation tests against the lesser grain borer showed little improvement in the toxicity of the glucosinolate breakdown products. Also, the 2-day and 4-day tests resulted in higher mortality in the control (data not shown).</p>
      <p>Little is known about the mode of action underlying the high fumigation toxicity of the glucosinolate breakdown products. Dichlorvos exerts its insecticidal toxicity against the insect nervous system, by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase. Methyl bromide and chloropicrin's insecticidal mode of action has been shown as their reactions with the nucleophilic sites such as OH, SH or NH<sub>2</sub> groups in vital enzymatic systems [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B25">25</xref>]. Many non-fumigant insecticides are known to affect the respiratory function of insects (e. g., rotenone, dinitrophenols) [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B20">20</xref>]. In our fumigation test technique, inhalation (intake through the spiracles) was the only way for the insects to receive the volatile chemicals, although certain degree of absorption could occur through the cuticle. Monitoring the CO<sub>2</sub> production of the insects at sub-lethal doses in a closed chamber may provide valuable symptomatic information toward understanding how these chemical volatiles affect the respiration of insects and lead to the eventual death of the insects. Monitoring CO<sub>2</sub> emission is one of the well established technique to study insect respiration and the effect of chemicals on the respiratory function, although it has sometimes been accompanied with simultaneous monitoring of oxygen consumption [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B24">24</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B26">26</xref>].</p>
      <p>The quantity of the chemicals used in our CO<sub>2</sub> test was based on the toxicity tests of the chemicals (data not shown). At the sub-lethal levels, no mortality was observed within the test period. For AIC, ATC and AITC, the amount of chemical utilized was 200 Î¼l of 10<sup>4</sup> ppm corn oil solution. This is equivalent to ca. 10 Î¼g cm<sup>-3</sup> of the chemical in air. This concentration is lethal to the lesser grain borer and the house fly, but not to the cockroaches due to different physiological aspects and the shorter exposure time in the CO<sub>2</sub> monitoring test.</p>
      <p>By exposing <italic>B. germanica</italic> to the fumes of the glucosinolate breakdown products AIC, ATC and AITC, we found that the CO<sub>2</sub> production of this insect was significantly increased at each time interval (Tables <xref ref-type="table" rid="T1">1</xref> and <xref ref-type="table" rid="T2">2</xref>, Fig. <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F2">2</xref>). AIC was the strongest CO<sub>2</sub> emission (respiration) enhancer. <italic>B. germanica</italic> treated with AIC produced more than 3 times more CO<sub>2</sub> than that in the control after being exposed for 1 h and 2 h. Even after 4 h, the CO<sub>2</sub> emission was more than 75% higher for AIC (Fig. <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F2">2</xref>). Although ATC and AITC did not increase the CO<sub>2</sub> emission as strongly as did AIC, these two chemicals still showed significant effects after 2 h. The effect was not as significant at 4 h for ATC and AITC (Fig. <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F2">2</xref>).</p>

      <p>Toxicity of isothiocyanates has been attributed to inactivation of the thiol group of essential enzymes [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B27">27</xref>]. The mode of action of many isothiocyanate compounds has also been attributed to their capability for alkylating the nucleophilic groups of biopolymers such as DNA, thus having cytotoxic properties which can affect the formation of the spiracular epidermis and crochet on the prolegs of tobacco hornworm (<italic>Manduca sexta</italic> L.) caterpillars [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B28">28</xref>-<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B30">30</xref>]. Our bioassay avoids totally any direct contact between the insects and the chemical, thus the insecticidal effect is very likely caused by inspiration of the chemical by the insects through their spiracles. The increased CO<sub>2</sub> emission observed in this study indicated a different mode of action of these glucosinolate breakdown products. This new mode of action may be similar to that of the dinitrophenols, although further investigation is needed to prove this speculation. Compounds such as dinitrophenol are uncouplers or inhibitors of oxidative phosphorylation [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B31">31</xref>]. When the coupling between the respiratory chain and phosphorylation is broken, respiratory control is lost, and the electron transport along the chain occurs at full speed without producing ATP, the high energy molecule. Uncouplers kill insects not by inhibiting normal electron transport of the respiratory chain, but rather by enhancing the respiration [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B27">27</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B31">31</xref>]. When the energy-transfer (production of ATP) is blocked, and at the same time, the respiration is accelerated, the insects have to use their stored energy sources and the exhaustion of those resources then lead directly to the death of the insects [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B31">31</xref>]. Insecticides such as rotenone and HCN are known to affect the respiratory function by inhibiting certain enzymes in the respiratory electron transport chain of insects, thus reducing the oxygen consumption and perhaps CO<sub>2</sub> production as well [<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="B20">20</xref>]. This may explain why HCN did not increase the CO<sub>2</sub> emission at low dose.</p>
      <p>As with many other conventional fumigants, we also found that some of the glucosinolate breakdown products and related chemicals were strong hatching inhibitors for the soybean cyst nematode (<italic>Heterodera glycines</italic>) (unpublished data, 1996)</p>
    </sec>
    <sec>
      <title>Conclusions</title>
      <p>Using glucosinolate breakdown products as safer fumigants in pest control is a novel approach in integrated pest management, and at the same time, since these natural products are considered fully biodegradable and less toxic, considering many cruciferous vegetables are consumed daily, there should be fewer environmental concerns. The bioactivity of these compounds may be from their effect on the insect respiratory system indicated by the inhibited or accelerated CO<sub>2</sub> production.</p>

        <title>Fumigation tests</title>
        <sec>
          <title>Fumigation of the House Fly</title>
          <p>Approximately 1 g 1/1 dry sucrose/powdered milk was placed in the bottom of a 40-ml jar, along with a 2-cm piece of cotton roll moistened with distilled water. Ten to 15 house fly adults (<italic>Musca domestica</italic> L., laboratory reared Orlando regular strain, one week post-emergence) were anesthetized with carbon dioxide and placed in the 40-ml jars. The jars were each covered with nylon mesh secured by a rubber band. Three 40 ml jars were placed in the bottom of a 2745 ml amber jar. A filter paper (Whatman No. 1, 9-cm diameter) was folded in quarters and placed in the jar as well. About 30 min after the flies recovered from anesthesia, 200 Î¼l of the appropriate corn oil (Flavoriteâ¢ pure corn oil, Preferred Products, Inc., Chaska, MN) solution of the chemicals was applied to the filter paper with an eppendorf pipette, the disposable tip of which was left in the jar. The control was treated under the same conditions with a corn oil blank. The above jar was tightly sealed and left for 24 h, when mortality was read. The insects were considered dead when they displayed no observable motion when prodded. LC<sub>50</sub> was calculated based on 5 concentrations.</p>
        </sec>
        <sec>
          <title>Fumigation of the lesser grain borer</title>
          <p>Ten adult lesser grain borers (<italic>Rhyzopertha dominica</italic> Fabricius, laboratory reared Manhattan KS strain) were placed in a 1.5 Ã 5-cm glass tube with 2 g whole wheat kernels. The tubes were closed on both ends by a tight metal mesh secured with parafilm. Three tubes were tied together with a wire, and suspended from the top of a jar (490 ml). A filter paper (same as used in the fly test), was folded in quarters and placed at the bottom of the jar. One hundred Î¼l of the appropriate corn oil dilution of the chemicals was applied to the filter paper with an eppendorf pipette, the disposable tip of which was left in the jar. Corn oil was used in the control. The jar was tightly capped and left for 24 h, at which time the mortality was recorded. An insect was considered dead when it displayed no observable motion upon prodding. LC<sub>50</sub> was calculated based on 5 concentrations.</p>
        </sec>
        <sec>
          <title>Measuring CO<sub>2</sub> emitted by insects</title>
          <p>We chose the German (<italic>Blattella germanica</italic> L.) and the American cockroach (<italic>Periplaneta americana</italic> L.) in this test because they are non-flying, thus producing a steady and measurable quantity of CO<sub>2</sub>. Two adult <italic>B. germanica</italic> (1 male, 1 female without ootheca) or 2 nymphs (average weight 0.55 g) of <italic>P. americana</italic> were weighed and put in a glass tube (5 Ã 1.5 cm) with both ends covered with mesh screen. A bigger size of tubing (5 Ã 2 cm) was used for the <italic>P. americana.</italic> The tube was then placed into a glass bottle (220 ml) together with a quarterly folded filter paper (same as used above). A rubber stopper with glass inlet and outlet was tightly capped on the bottle with both ends sealed with a rubber septum (Fig <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F1">1</xref>). An infrared carbon dioxide analyzer (LIRA<sup>Â®</sup> Infrared gas analyzer, Model 3000, Mine Safety Appliances Company, Pittsburgh, PA) was used to measure the emitted CO<sub>2</sub>. The sealed bottle was flushed with CO<sub>2</sub>-free air (compressed air filtered through soda lime tubes and a NaOH trap) before introducing the chemical. A sub-lethal dose of the breakdown products (200 Î¼l of 10000 ppm chemical in corn oil for the <italic>B. germanica,</italic> and 100 Î¼l of 1000 ppm for <italic>P. americana</italic>) was immediately injected with a syringe through the septum onto the filter paper inside the system (Fig. <xref ref-type="fig" rid="F1">1</xref>). In this system, direct contact between the insects and the chemical is completely avoided. The CO<sub>2</sub> emitted by the test insects was recorded at Range 1 (the most sensitive range) of the instrument at different exposure times. There were three replicates for each treatment unless stated otherwise, and the same volume of corn oil was used in the control. Tests on the effect of HCN were achieved by wetting the filter paper with 100 Î¼l of 1 N HCl, and, after flushing out the CO<sub>2</sub>, injecting 20 Î¼l of 1 N NaCN aqueous solution onto the paper. Rotenone (5 Î¼l of 100 ppm in acetone) was injected to the thorax of the <italic>P. americana</italic> nymph in the CO<sub>2</sub> test and same the amount of acetone was used in the control. Water or acetone was used in the control for HCN or rotenone.</p>
